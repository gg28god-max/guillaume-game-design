VR UI · Spatial Interfaces · Concept Art · 2025

Wings of Wanitu

A deep dive into immersive, spatial user interfaces highlighting the unique design challenges of Virtual Reality. As Lead on UI architecture, this project explores diegetic cockpit interfaces and glider dashboard localization systems optimized for VR performance — alongside a full suite of enemy creature designs, from concept through behaviour sheets and final color passes.

Year

2025

Role

Lead UI Architect & Concept Artist

Disciplines

VR UI, Spatial Interfaces, Enemy Design, World-Building

03 — Art Brief

The Brief

Wings of Wanitu asked me to solve two different design problems that happened to share a cockpit: an enemy needs to read as a threat before the player consciously processes it, and a HUD needs to disappear into peripheral vision until the moment it's needed. The brief below is what I held both halves of the project to.

Premise

A VR aerial rogue-like for Meta Quest and SteamVR — high-speed flight combat across floating island biomes, against a bestiary of organic and automated adversaries. Built at Little Guy Games; my brief covered both the enemy cast and the cockpit itself.

Mood & Tone

Stylized over photoreal — painterly skies and creature silhouettes that read like concept art, not simulation. The cockpit stays high-contrast and diegetic-adjacent rather than screen-accurate, since clarity at speed matters more than realism.

Deliverables

A full enemy roster (Lurkers, Interceptors, Fighters) taken from silhouette through behaviour sheets to final color pass, plus the hero glider's cockpit dashboard: a 360-degree spherical radar, health/energy rings, and mission/currency HUD panels.

Design Constraints

Every enemy design had to double as a gameplay signal — flying pattern, attack telegraph, and scale all had to read correctly before a full rig existed. Every UI element had to hold up in a stereoscopic headset at arm's length, where flat contrast and fine text simply don't work.

04 — The Problem

The Problem I Set Out to Solve

Making a threat readable before it's understood

In VR, there's no pause to study an enemy. The Interceptor's attack telegraph — wings and fins physically collapsing before it charges its electric blast — had to be legible from a glance and from any angle, because a player's reaction window in a headset is shorter than on a flat screen. The behaviour sheets exist because the silhouette alone had to carry that information.

A HUD that works when the whole world is the screen

A traditional 2D HUD doesn't work in VR — there's no fixed frame to anchor it to, and stacking flat panels in 3D space breaks immersion fast. The spherical radar and ring-based health/energy display went through several visual passes (the dashboard sheet shows three) before landing on a version legible in peripheral vision without pulling focus from flight.

Two disciplines, one deadline, no handoff

Leading both the creature art and the UI architecture meant neither could be treated as the "other" team's problem. A cockpit redesign had to account for what the enemies needed to communicate, and an enemy repaint had to stay readable against whatever the HUD was doing in the same frame — there was no wall between the two halves of the brief.
